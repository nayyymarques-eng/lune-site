Citrine is the colour of a lit window on a cold evening. It is a yellow to golden orange quartz, one of the most loved stones on home altars, and it is also the birthstone of November. This guide covers what citrine means, where it comes from, how to tell what kind you have, how to care for it, and a simple new moon ritual with citrine at its centre.

## What citrine is good for

- **A focus for warmth and confidence.** Its sunny colour makes it a natural reminder of what you want more of.
- **New beginnings.** It suits the new moon and the growing nights after it, when you name something you want to build.
- **A practice around enough.** Many people keep citrine where they think about money and work, as a reminder to look at what they have with gratitude, not fear.
- **Carrying an intention.** It is small and sturdy enough to keep in a pocket for weeks, so the ritual stays with you after the evening ends.

None of this happens in the stone. Citrine gives your intention a shape and a colour you can hold, and that is its whole gift.

## What citrine is

Citrine is quartz, the same mineral as clear rock crystal and purple amethyst. Its yellow comes from traces of iron in the crystal. On the Mohs scale it is a 7, harder than glass, with good toughness, so it is durable enough to wear every day and to handle in a ritual for years.

The name is usually traced to the French word *citron*, lemon, and through it to the Latin *citrina*, yellow. Its colours run from pale lemon through honey and gold to a deep orange or reddish brown.

## Where citrine comes from

People have worn quartz for thousands of years, and citrine has been reported in Roman jewellery. It became especially loved in the nineteenth century, when it was set in the colourful Scottish brooches and jewellery of the Victorian era.

For most of that history citrine lived in the shadow of yellow topaz. The two look alike, and the names were used for each other for centuries. So citrine inherited topaz's lore: people believed it could soothe the temper and calm whoever wore it. In the twentieth century, citrine was added beside topaz as the birthstone of November, and it is the gem of the thirteenth wedding anniversary.

Today the best known source of natural citrine is the Anahí mine in Bolivia, deep in the Pantanal wetland, where single crystals hold both purple and yellow quartz (cut together, they are called ametrine). Natural citrine also comes from Spain, Madagascar, Mexico and Uruguay.

## Natural or heated: what kind is yours

Natural citrine is rare. Most citrine sold today began as [amethyst](/journal/amethyst-meaning), mostly from Brazil, and was heated until the purple turned to yellow or orange. This is a long accepted practice in the gem trade, and it does not make the stone fake. It is still quartz.

A few signs help you tell them apart:

- **Heated citrine** is often a strong orange, amber or reddish brown, sometimes with a paler, almost white base where a geode cluster grew.
- **Natural citrine** is usually a soft, even lemon or smoky yellow, often lighter than people expect.
- **Very cheap, bright orange clusters** are almost always heated amethyst.

On an altar, both hold the same place. What makes a stone yours is the attention you give it, not the temperature it once met.

## How to care for citrine

- **Clean it** with warm, soapy water and a soft brush, then dry it with a cloth.
- **Avoid high heat** and sudden changes of temperature, which can crack quartz. No steam cleaning, no radiators.
- **Sun in moderation.** A morning on a sunny window sill is a lovely gesture. Days of hot, direct sun are not needed and are not kind to any stone.
- **Store it apart** from softer stones like selenite or moonstone, which citrine can scratch.

Many people like to cleanse a stone before a ritual. You can do it with gestures that are gentle on quartz: a rinse in cool running water, a night on the window sill under the moon, or a few minutes held in the smoke of an incense you love, indoors with a window open.

## When to work with citrine

Citrine fits the **new moon** and the waxing nights after it. The new moon is the start of the cycle, a night for naming what you want to grow, and a yellow stone is a natural companion for that. The next new moon is on **Monday 9 November 2026**, in Scorpio. Our [guide to the new moon in Scorpio](/journal/new-moon-in-scorpio-2026) has its meaning and a ritual of its own. The moon then grows until the [full supermoon in Gemini](/journal/full-supermoon-in-gemini-2026) on 24 November, a good night to look back at what you planted.

Mornings suit citrine too. Its colour belongs to daylight, and a short practice by a window at sunrise is one of the simplest ways to keep it.

## What you need

- **A citrine**, natural or heated, any size you can close your hand around.
- **A yellow or orange candle**, for indoors only, in a stable holder on a heat-safe surface.
- **An incense tied to warmth**, such as cinnamon, orange peel or frankincense, or a small bowl of dried orange peel if you prefer no smoke.
- **A small dish** of something golden from your kitchen: a few grains of rice, a cinnamon stick, a slice of dried orange.
- **Paper and a pen.**
- **A small bowl of water** for the end of the ritual.

Arrange them in a small circle on your altar or a cleared table, the citrine in the middle.

## A new moon ritual with citrine, step by step

### 1. Arrive

Light the candle and the incense, indoors, with a window open a little. Sit where you can see the flame. Take three slow breaths, longer on the out breath than the in breath. Let your shoulders drop.

### 2. Hold the stone

Pick up the citrine and close your hand around it. Notice its weight, how cool it is, how quickly it warms. Look at its colour in the candlelight. This is the moment of arriving fully: one small, bright thing in your hand.

### 3. Name what you want to grow

On the paper, write one sentence that begins *"This moon, I want to grow…"*. Keep it about you and your own life: warmth at home, courage at work, a calmer way of looking at money, more joy in an ordinary week. If you like, write it as an intention to attract something: *"I set my intention to attract more calm into my days."* Read it aloud once, slowly.

### 4. Name what you already have

Under it, write three things you already have that belong to the same wish. If you want more warmth, write where you already find it. If you want enough, write three things that are already enough. Citrine's old nickname in crystal practice, the merchant's stone, is easier to live with when it starts from gratitude.

### 5. Seal it

Fold the paper around the citrine, or lay the stone on top of it, and set both beside the dish of golden things. Hold your hands over them for a few breaths.

### 6. Close

Dip your fingertips in the bowl of water and touch them to your closed eyes, as a way of saying the evening is complete. Thank the moon, and yourself for making the time. Put the candle out with a snuffer or a lid; never leave it burning.

Keep the citrine with the folded paper on your altar until the full moon, or carry it in a pocket. Each time you notice it, read the sentence again in your head. At the full moon on 24 November, open the paper and see what has grown.

## Why a gesture like this helps

There is no study showing that a stone changes your life, and Lune will never tell you otherwise. There is good research on what rituals do. In a series of experiments, people who performed a short ritual before a stressful task felt less anxious and performed better than those who did not, and the effect depended on the gestures being framed as a ritual. A review of the psychology of rituals suggests they help people regulate emotion, focus on goals and feel connected. Writing a feeling or a wish in words also seems to help: in one brain imaging study, putting feelings into words calmed activity in the amygdala, the brain's alarm.

A citrine in your pocket works in a similar, humble way. It is a cue. Every time your fingers find it, your attention returns to what you chose on the night of the new moon.
