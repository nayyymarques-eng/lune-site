Samhain is kept on the night of **Saturday 31 October 2026**. It is the old Gaelic feast at the turn of the year, when the harvest is in, the days shorten fast and the dark half of the year begins. Modern witchcraft keeps it as the first of the eight festivals of the Wheel of the Year, and many people of other paths keep it simply as a night of remembrance.

This guide covers what Samhain is good for, where it comes from, and five gentle rituals you can keep at home, each with what you need and the steps.

## What Samhain is good for

- **Remembering someone you love.** The heart of the night: a name said aloud, a photo by the light, a memory shared.
- **Closing the light half of the year.** A pause to notice what this year grew and what has ended.
- **Turning toward rest.** The harvest is in; the season asks for slower evenings, warmth and home.
- **Gathering the people around you.** A shared meal and stories by the light are among its oldest customs.
- **Marking the season with your own hands.** Apples, leaves, a lantern, an altar that changes with the year.

## What Samhain means and where it comes from

**Samhain** is the Irish name for November and for the feast that opened it. In medieval Ireland it was one of the great assemblies of the year, when the herds came down from the summer pastures, the harvest was counted and the community gathered. Irish and Scottish sources tell of fires lit on the hills and of the night as a threshold between one half of the year and the other (Hutton, 1996).

Over the centuries, the eve of All Hallows on 31 October and the old feast shared the same night. In Ireland and Scotland people went guising, visiting houses in costume, and carried lanterns carved from turnips. Emigrants took the custom to North America, where the turnip became the pumpkin.

In the twentieth century, **modern witchcraft** took Samhain as one of its eight sabbats, the festivals of the Wheel of the Year, and keeps it as a night to remember those who came before. In Lune it is remembrance only, never calling anyone back.

The same gesture lives far from Ireland. **In Mexico**, on the Day of the Dead at the start of November, families set out photos, candles, marigolds and the favourite food of the people they love. Late autumn is a time of remembrance in many places, and the gestures are kin: a light, a face, something good to eat.

## When to keep Samhain in 2026

In the northern hemisphere, the night is **Saturday 31 October**, and many people keep it through 1 November. Any evening from the 30th to the 2nd carries the same meaning.

The moon that night is waning, a few days after the full moon of 26 October and a day before the last quarter on 1 November. In the lunar cycle, the waning moon is the time to rest, give thanks and let go, the same turn Samhain makes in the year. If you want to know where the moon falls in your own chart that night, the [moon compass](/tonight) shows it.

In the **southern hemisphere** the wheel turns the other way: most people keep Samhain on 1 May and celebrate Beltane, the opening of the bright half, on 31 October.

## 5 gentle Samhain rituals

Choose one, or keep them together through the evening. Candles and lanterns stay indoors on a steady plate, away from curtains, and are never left burning when you leave the room.

### 1. A Samhain remembrance candle

The centre of the night: a light for someone you carry with you.

**You need:** a white candle on a plate (or a warm lamp), a photo or the written name of someone you love who is no longer here, a seasonal fruit or their favourite food, and a stone you like, if you have one.

1. Breathe in for a count of four and out for a count of eight, a few times, until the room feels still.
2. Light the candle and set the photo or the name beside it.
3. Say their name out loud.
4. Tell them, or the room, one memory of them that makes you smile.
5. Thank them for one thing they gave you that you still carry.
6. Eat the fruit slowly in their memory, then put the candle out and say: "I remember you, and I carry what you gave me."

### 2. A memory meal

Food shared in memory is one of the oldest ways to remember.

**You need:** a recipe that belongs to someone you love, the people you want around the table, and one empty moment in the meal.

1. Cook the dish they made, or the one they loved. If you don't have the recipe, cook something from the place they came from.
2. Before eating, let each person say the name of someone they are remembering tonight.
3. Halfway through the meal, ask one question and let everyone answer: "What did they teach you?"
4. Write the recipe down, with the stories told tonight, and keep it.

### 3. An autumn altar

A small place that holds the season and the people in it.

**You need:** a cloth, apples or other seasonal fruit, fallen leaves, nuts, a small squash or gourd, a candle, and a photo or a name.

1. Clear a shelf or a corner of a table and lay the cloth.
2. Set out what the season gave you: fruit, leaves, nuts, the squash.
3. Add the photo or the name, and the candle beside them.
4. Leave the altar up until the next full moon on 24 November, and give the fruit back to the soil or the table before it spoils.

Our guide to [moon water](/journal/moon-water) shows how to add a small bowl of water kept under the moon.

### 4. A letter to the closing year

Samhain closes the light half of the year. This is a way to close it on the page.

**You need:** a page and a pen, and a little time alone.

1. Write what this year grew for you: three things, small or large.
2. Write what has ended, or what you are ready to let end.
3. Write one thing you want to keep warm through the dark months.
4. Fold the page and keep it until Imbolc, the first stirring of spring on 1 February, and read it again then.

### 5. A lantern for the dark half

The carved turnip lanterns of Ireland and Scotland lit the way home on this night.

**You need:** a turnip, a small pumpkin or a jar, a knife or spoon for carving, and a battery tea light.

1. Hollow the turnip or pumpkin, or wrap the jar in paper.
2. Carve or draw a simple shape: a moon, a leaf, a face.
3. Set the battery light inside and place the lantern in a window.
4. Each time you see it tonight, think of one person who helped light your way this year.

## Why rituals of remembrance help

Remembrance can feel heavy, and research suggests that a small ritual can make it easier to hold.

- **Rituals ease loss.** In experiments with real losses, people who did a small ritual afterwards reported less grief and more sense of control than people who did not (Norton & Gino, 2014).
- **Keeping the bond is healthy.** Grief researchers now see keeping a bond with someone we lost, through memory, stories and objects, as a healthy part of grieving rather than something to close off (Klass, Silverman & Nickman, 1996).
- **Writing helps to hold it.** Writing about feelings we keep to ourselves, over a few days, went with fewer visits to the doctor in the months after, in a small study (Pennebaker & Beall, 1986). It can stir feelings at first, so go gently.

Studies suggest; they do not promise. If a loss is recent or feels too heavy, go slowly, keep only the parts that feel kind, and reach out to someone you trust.

## The rest of the Wheel of the Year

Samhain is the first of eight seasonal days in the Wheel of the Year, roughly six weeks apart. After it come Yule at the winter solstice, Imbolc on 1 February, Ostara at the spring equinox, Beltane on 1 May, Litha at the summer solstice, Lughnasadh on 1 August and Mabon at the autumn equinox. Each marks a turn of the light, and each has its own gesture.
