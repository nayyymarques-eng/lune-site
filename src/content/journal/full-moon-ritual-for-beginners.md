The full moon is the one night of the month when the whole sky seems to agree to look at the same thing. People have gathered under it for as long as there are records, to give thanks, to settle what is unfinished and to let something go. You do not need a special altar or anything to buy to keep it. You need a little time, a few things you already have, and a reason that is yours.

This guide walks through the ritual gesture by gesture, with where each gesture comes from and what research says about it.

## What the full moon means

At the full moon, the Sun and the moon stand on opposite sides of the Earth. The moon rises as the Sun sets, stays up all night and sets as the Sun rises. For a few nights the dark has no corner the moon does not reach.

That is the meaning most traditions give it: everything is visible now, the beautiful and the unfinished. The new moon, two weeks earlier, was the seed; the full moon shows what the seed became. It is a night to see clearly, to give thanks for what grew and to release what has already ended.

## How people have kept the full moon

Keeping the full moon is one of the most widely shared habits there is.

- **In China**, the Mid-Autumn Festival falls on the fifteenth day of the eighth lunar month, a full moon. Families gather, share round mooncakes the shape of the moon and look up together, even when they live far apart.
- **In Buddhist countries**, uposatha days are kept at the full and new moon. Many people visit the temple, renew their precepts and spend the day more quietly. Vesak, the most important of the year, falls on a full moon.
- **In India**, each full moon is a Purnima, many with their own observance. Guru Purnima honours teachers; Kartik Purnima fills rivers with small floating lamps.
- **In Japan**, tsukimi, the autumn moon viewing, sets out rice dumplings and pampas grass by a window that faces the moon.
- **In modern Wicca and paganism**, the full moon gathering is called an esbat, often held as a circle of friends.

Different gestures, one habit: on the brightest night of the month, stop, gather and look at what the light shows.

## When to do your full moon ritual

The full moon is exact at one moment, given to the minute in any moon calendar. To the eye, the moon looks full for about three nights around it. Any of those evenings works: the night of the exact moment, or the one before or after, whichever is clear and whichever you can keep without hurry.

The best hour is the first part of the night. The full moon rises close to sunset, so a little after dark it sits low on the horizon, large and golden, and easy to find from a window or a doorstep. If the sky is cloudy, keep the ritual anyway. The moon is the reminder; the gestures are the practice.

## What you need

Nothing you need to buy. Choose a few things that already mean something to you. A full moon ritual usually has something to look at, something to write with, and something to hold or release.

- **A page and a pen**, for the looking back and the letting go.
- **A light**: a candle, an oil lamp or a small lamp you only use for this. Keep any flame on a steady plate, away from fabric, and never leave it alone.
- **Something that holds**: a stone you picked up somewhere, a cord or a ribbon you can tie, a sprig of rosemary or bay from the kitchen.
- **Something that clears**: a small bowl of water, or a pinch of salt on a saucer. Salt and water have been used to mark a clean start in many homes and many faiths.
- **Something that is yours**: a photo, a figure of your faith, a shell, a letter. This is what turns a table into an altar.

None of this is required. A window and a page are enough. The objects are there because the hand remembers what it has touched.

## A full moon ritual, step by step

Take your time with each step. There is no clock to beat.

### 1. Prepare the space

Choose one spot: a windowsill, a corner of a table, a step outside. Put your phone in another room. Lay out what you chose and light the lamp or candle. The simple act of setting things in their place tells the rest of the day it is over.

### 2. Arrive in your body

Sit comfortably. Breathe in through the nose, then take a second, shorter breath in on top of it, and let it all out slowly through the mouth. This is called a physiological sigh. Repeat it until your shoulders drop, usually a few minutes.

### 3. Look at the moon

If you can see it, look at the moon for a while without doing anything else. Notice its colour, the darker seas on its face, how high it sits. If it is behind clouds, look at the light on your table instead. Just look.

### 4. Look back and give thanks

On the page, write what this month gave you. Start with three things and let more come if they want to. Small is welcome: a conversation, a meal, a morning you liked. Thanks is the oldest full moon gesture, and the one most traditions share.

### 5. Name what has ended

Below a line, write one thing that has already ended: a habit, a worry, a role, a story you keep telling about yourself. Not what you wish would end, what already has. The full moon is not for forcing, it is for seeing what is true.

### 6. Release it

Choose the gesture that feels right to you:

- Tear that part of the page away and fold it small.
- Tie a knot in a cord while you hold it in mind, then cut the knot loose.
- Put the paper in the bowl of water and watch the ink soften.
- Keep it to bury in soil tomorrow, under a plant or a tree.
- If you burn it, do it outside, in a fireproof bowl, with water close.

### 7. Close

Look at the moon or the light one more time. Say thank you, out loud or to yourself, to whoever you thank. Put out the flame. Keep the half of the page with your thanks; it is worth reading again at the next new moon.

## Variations for different needs

The same shape holds many intentions. Change only step 5 and step 6.

- **To let go after a hard month:** write what you are carrying that is not yours, and release only that.
- **For a relationship that changed:** write what you learned from it and what you are grateful for, then fold the page and keep it rather than destroy it.
- **For work:** write the effort you made that nobody saw. Recognise it before you release anything.
- **With friends, as a full moon circle:** each person brings one object for a shared altar. You breathe together, write alone, and each says aloud only one line of thanks.

## Why it helps: what research says

The moon itself does not appear to change how we feel or sleep in any large, reliable way. A 2021 study found people fell asleep a little later in the nights before a full moon (Casiraghi et al.), while large re-analyses of sleep data have found no effect (Cordi et al., 2014). The evidence is mixed, and Lune does not lean on it.

What the research does support are the gestures inside the ritual:

- **Rituals themselves.** People who performed a short ritual after a loss reported less grief than those who did not (Norton & Gino, 2014). Rituals before a stressful task lowered anxiety and heart rate (Brooks et al., 2016).
- **The breath.** Five minutes a day of cyclic sighing improved mood and lowered breathing rate more than mindfulness meditation over a month (Balban et al., 2023).
- **Giving thanks in writing.** Keeping a gratitude list over several weeks went with more wellbeing and better sleep (Emmons & McCullough, 2003).
- **Throwing the page away.** People who wrote down a negative thought and then threw the paper in a bin relied on that thought less afterwards than people who kept it (Briñol et al., 2013).
- **Looking at something vast.** Short walks that invited people to look for awe raised joy and warmth toward others over eight weeks (Sturm et al., 2022). A full moon is one of the easiest vast things to find.

The moon gives the gesture a time and a reason. The gesture does the rest.

## Making it yours

Keep what works and leave what does not. Some people keep every full moon page in one notebook and read the year back in December. Some keep the same stone on the altar and add one object a month. Some only look up, say thank you and go to bed. Each is a full way to keep the full moon.
