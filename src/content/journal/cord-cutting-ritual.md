Some things stay with us long after they have ended. A relationship that is over, a job we left, a version of ourselves we have outgrown, a worry we keep carrying because we have carried it for so long. A cord cutting ritual gives that feeling a shape you can hold, a thread, and a gesture you can make, a cut. This guide covers what a cord cutting ritual is, when it helps, what you need, and a gentle practice for the waning moon, step by step.

## What a cord cutting ritual is good for

- **Letting go after something ends.** A breakup, a move, a friendship that faded, a chapter that closed.
- **Releasing a habit or a thought loop.** Checking a phone, replaying an argument, an old story about yourself.
- **Putting down a role you no longer need.** Always being the one who fixes things, or the one who is never enough.
- **Taking back your attention.** The ritual marks a clear before and after, so the mind has a moment to point to.
- **Making room before a new moon.** Clearing what you carry makes space for what you want to begin.

None of this happens in the thread. The thread gives a decision a shape, and the cut gives it a moment. The letting go is yours, and it often keeps unfolding for weeks after the evening.

## What cord cutting means

The image is simple. Whatever we are attached to feels as though it is joined to us by an invisible cord. Some cords are kind: love, friendship, the place we grew up. Others pull: guilt, resentment, a hope that keeps us waiting. The ritual does not cut what is kind. It names the part that pulls and lets it go.

There are many ways to believe, and each one is whole. Some people feel these cords as something real between people. Others see them as a picture of how the mind holds on. Both can meet in this practice, because the gesture is the same: you see what you are holding, and you choose to open your hand.

One rule matters more than any other. **A cord cutting ritual is only ever about you.** You cannot cut a cord for someone else, and you never try to change what another person thinks, feels or does. You release your own side: your waiting, your worry, your old story. That is the only end of any cord that is yours.

## When to do it

The **waning moon** is the traditional time for release. After the full moon the light grows less each night, and many people use these two weeks to clear, rest and let go. The last nights before a new moon, the waning crescent, are especially fitting. Our guide to the [waning crescent moon](/journal/waning-crescent-moon) has more on this phase.

In 2026 the moon wanes until the **new moon in Scorpio on Monday 9 November**, then again from the full supermoon on 24 November until the new moon on 9 December. A cord cutting on the waning moon followed by a [new moon ritual](/journal/new-moon-ritual) a few days later is a natural pair: first you clear, then you begin. The [new moon in Scorpio](/journal/new-moon-in-scorpio-2026), a sign of endings and renewal, suits it especially well.

Choose an evening when you will not be interrupted, and when you feel steady enough to sit with whatever comes up. If you are in the first raw days of a loss, it is fine to wait. The ritual will still be here.

## What you need

- **A length of natural thread or string**, about as long as your forearm. Cotton, wool or twine all work. Choose a colour that feels right to you: black or grey for endings, white for a clean start, or simply what you have.
- **Scissors.** Sharp enough to cut in one clean move.
- **Paper and a pen.**
- **A small bowl of water**, and a pinch of salt if you like.
- **A candle**, for indoors only, in a stable holder on a heat-safe surface, kept apart from the thread and the paper. Or a small lamp, if you prefer no flame.
- **Something for the senses**, if you like: an incense such as cedar or frankincense, a sprig of rosemary, a dried bay leaf, a smooth stone to hold.
- **A small cloth or tray** to set everything on, or your home altar if you keep one.

## A cord cutting ritual for the waning moon, step by step

### 1. Prepare the space

Tidy the table or altar and set everything out. Wash your hands slowly in warm water, as a sign that you are arriving. Light the candle, or the lamp, and the incense if you are using one, indoors with a window open a little. Put your phone in another room.

### 2. Arrive

Sit comfortably. Take five slow breaths, letting the out breath be longer than the in breath. Feel your feet on the floor and the weight of your body on the chair. Nothing needs to happen yet.

### 3. Name what you are releasing

On the paper, write one sentence that begins *"I am ready to let go of…"*. Keep it about you: *the habit of waiting for a message*, *the guilt I still carry about leaving*, *the belief that I have to fix everything*. If a person comes to mind, write what *you* are holding about them, never what you want them to do.

Under it, write a few lines about what this cord has cost you, and what it once gave you. Both are true. Write until it feels said.

### 4. Hold the thread

Take the thread in both hands, one end in each. Let it stand for the cord you named. Pull it gently taut and notice where you feel it in your body: the chest, the throat, the stomach. Stay with it for a few breaths. You are not trying to make the feeling go away. You are letting yourself see it clearly.

### 5. Say what you choose

Out loud or in your head, say a sentence of your own, or this one:

*"I thank what this was. I take back what is mine. I let the rest go."*

Speak slowly. These are not magic words. They are a way of telling yourself, in plain language, what you have decided.

### 6. Cut

Hold the thread over the bowl and cut it in one clean move. Let the two pieces fall. Take a long breath out as they drop.

Notice how you feel. Relief, sadness, nothing at all, all of these are part of it. Many people feel a little lighter. Some feel a wave of grief first. Both are normal.

### 7. Rinse

Dip your fingertips in the bowl of water and touch your wrists, as a way of rinsing the evening off. If you added salt, the old habit of salt for cleansing belongs to many places and times, and here it is simply a gesture of clearing.

### 8. Close

Fold the paper. Thank the moon, or whatever you feel is bigger than you, and yourself for making the time. Put the candle out with a snuffer or a lid; never leave a flame burning when you leave the room.

## After the ritual

- **Let the pieces go.** The next morning, put the cut thread in the bin or the compost, and pour the water into the soil of a plant or a garden. You can tear the paper into small pieces and recycle it, or keep it in a journal to read again at the next moon. Do not burn it at home.
- **Be gentle for a few days.** Drink water, sleep, walk, spend time with people who feel easy. Letting go can be tiring.
- **Notice the old pull.** When the habit or thought comes back, and it often will, picture the cut thread and take one slow breath out. Each time is a small repeat of the ritual.
- **Repeat if you need to.** Some cords take a few moons. On the next waning moon, you can do the ritual again, lighter.

If what you are carrying feels too heavy to hold alone, a cord cutting ritual is not a replacement for help. Talking to a friend, a counsellor or a doctor is part of letting go too.

## Variations

- **A meditation only.** No objects at all. Sit, breathe, picture the cord between you and what you are releasing, and see it gently loosen and fall away. Many people start here.
- **Cutting a cord to a place.** After a move, take a thread from home to your new door, then cut it on the doorstep, keeping one piece as a thank you to the old place.
- **Cutting a cord to a habit.** Name the habit, cut the thread, and put one small new thing in its place the next day, such as a walk at the hour you used to scroll.
- **With a bath.** Take a warm bath before the ritual with a handful of salt and a few sprigs of rosemary, as a way to arrive. Keep candles in the bathroom on a stable, dry surface, away from towels.

## Why a gesture like this helps

There is no study that measures invisible cords, and Lune will never promise that a ritual changes your life. There is good research on what rituals and writing do for people who are letting go. In a set of experiments, people who performed a simple ritual after a loss, including the end of a relationship, reported less grief and a greater sense of control than those who did not. Other experiments found that a short ritual before a stressful task lowered anxiety. Decades of studies on expressive writing suggest that putting difficult experiences into words helps people make sense of them and can ease their weight over time. And in one brain imaging study, naming a feeling in words calmed activity in the amygdala, the brain's alarm.

A cord cutting ritual brings these together in one humble gesture: you name what you hold, you write it down, you make a clear moment of choice. The thread is a cue. The letting go is something you do, slowly, and the ritual gives it a place to start.
